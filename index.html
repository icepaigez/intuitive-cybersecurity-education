They Didn't Know They Were Learning: an Intuitive Approach to Cybersecurity Education

Abstract

What if the most effective way to teach a technical concept is to not teach it at all?

That question emerged from a practical observation, not a theoretical premise. A competition designed to teach security thinking to nineteen secondary schools produced something unexpected: students were engaging - but through pattern matching, not understanding. The activity worked on the surface and failed underneath. That gap between intended outcome and observed behaviour prompted a research inquiry that led to the Intuitive Cybersecurity Education (ICE) framework.

ICE is built on a single proposition: that a game whose internal mechanics are structurally identical to the real system being taught will build transferable, procedural knowledge - in learners who do not know they are being taught. The framework does not supplement instruction with games. It replaces instruction with consequential play, and allows knowledge to emerge from the doing rather than the telling.

Signal is the first game deliberately built on ICE principles. Players work inbound and outbound on a live relay strip - decoding survivor locations and, after the opening arc, encoding short acknowledgements - across binary, hexadecimal, ASCII, Base64, and URL representations because survival demands it, not because a lesson names the formats first. Player-facing relay copy avoids encoding jargon; vocabulary and worked examples belong in the debrief after play. The knowledge emerges. The instruction comes after.

The ICE framework is theoretically grounded in established learning science and designed to address the transfer failure that existing approaches - including well-intentioned game-based ones - consistently produce. Signal is its first deliberate implementation. Whether it achieves what the theory predicts is the question this paper raises, and a planned controlled evaluation will answer.
